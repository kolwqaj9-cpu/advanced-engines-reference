## 1. Researches on direct injection in internal-combustion engines

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930094424>

NACA Technical Memorandum 993 preserves an early systematic study of direct-injection combustion, built around Lehmann-Zeiss combustion cards recorded at 700 rpm on a small single-cylinder engine. The report varies injection pressure, nozzle hole diameter (0.20 to 0.30 mm), injection duration, and charge, and reads the results off indicator cards and fuel-consumption runs. The working findings that survive cleanly: for an identical injection pressure, specific fuel consumption falls as injection becomes faster; the economical power of the engine — the power at minimum specific consumption — rises with both speed and injection pressure; and at low injection pressure the spray lacks penetration and dispersion, so the atomization cones fill only part of the combustion chamber and both power and economy suffer. Recorded minima in the card series run from roughly 230 g/hp/h down to about 189 to 197 g/hp/h as injection pressure is raised from about 310 atm toward 610 atm, with the engine idling cleanly at 200 rpm in the better configurations. The memorandum remains a useful baseline for how injection pressure, timing, and nozzle geometry trade against each other in a direct-injection chamber.

## 2. Liquid sprays and flow studies in the direct-injection diesel engine under motored conditions

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19880009210>

This NASA study reports computed and measured spray and flow fields inside a motored direct-injection diesel geometry. The engine variables investigated are tabulated directly in the report: swirl angle, spray angle, injection angle, peak of the droplet distribution function, and maximum droplet diameter, with intake port geometry given in inches. The results are presented as crank-angle sequences — fuel mass fraction distributions at 350°, 360°, 10°, 30°, and 60° after top dead center; oxygen mass fraction distributions as a function of swirl angle; Sauter mean diameter distributions; temperature isocontours in degrees Kelvin; gas-phase velocity vector profiles; and natural logarithm of turbulent kinetic energy profiles — each computed for swirl angles of 0°, 11.25°, 22.5°, 30°, and 45°. The value of the report for a working engineer is the direct comparison it permits between swirl levels: it shows how swirl redistributes the fuel cloud, where the dense core persists, and how droplet size and turbulence fields respond as the piston passes top dead center.

## 3. § 1036.104 Criteria pollutant emission standards

Primary source: <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/part-1036>

Title 40, Part 1036 of the Code of Federal Regulations sets the criteria-pollutant emission standards for heavy-duty highway engines, and § 1036.550 gives the reference fuel properties used when correcting emission results. The reference fuel table is reproduced here because it is the piece working engineers reach for: diesel fuel carries a carbon-mass-specific net energy content of 49.3112 MJ/kgC and a carbon mass fraction of 0.874; gasoline 50.4742 MJ/kgC and 0.846; natural gas 66.2910 MJ/kgC and 0.750; LPG 56.5218 MJ/kgC and 0.820; dimethyl ether 55.3886 MJ/kgC and 0.521; high-level ethanol-gasoline blends 50.3211 MJ/kgC and 0.576. For fuels not listed, the regulation requires EPA approval of the reference properties, and for multi-fuel streams such as natural gas with diesel pilot injection it directs the user to determine blended values with good engineering judgment. The worked example in the same paragraph shows a measured CO2 result of 630.0 g/hp-hr corrected to 624.5 g/hp-hr. Because the eCFR is the current regulatory text, the link above always resolves to the edition now in force.

## 4. Small engine technology programs

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19920013289>

This NASA Lewis briefing summarizes small-engine technology work of the late 1980s. The ceramic turbine effort completed the aerodynamic design of a high-work, high-temperature ceramic mixed-flow turbine and the structural analysis of the first design, using NASTRAN capability with Weibull statistical failure criteria from fracture mechanics; the briefing notes the need for improved materials and further code validation, and identifies Lewis as the only government facility with an in-house capability to design ceramic components. The cooled radial turbine work with Allison reports internal coolant heat transfer coefficients for rotor inlet flow at several percent of passage flow. The rotary engine line shows a first test against a design objective of 160 hp at 8,000 rpm with 0.35 BSFC, and demonstrated 160 hp at 8,000 rpm, with follow-on work on high-speed electronic fuel control, turbocompounding, adiabatic components, and a lightweight reduced-friction rotor. A companion diesel objective aimed to enlarge the diesel technology base for a wide range of air and land vehicles in the 500 to 2,000 hp class, focusing on high speed and high pressure to reduce size, with an expected fuel saving of 40 percent from a compact compound cycle.

## 5. Performance and emissions characteristics of aqueous alcohol fumes in a DI diesel engine

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19830003980>

This report examines ethanol and methanol introduced as aqueous fumes into a direct-injection diesel. The fuel property tables are among its most-used contents: at 68°F, specific gravity is 0.795 for ethanol, 0.796 for methanol, and about 0.846 for No. 2 fuel oil; liquid density runs 6.60 and 6.61 lbm/gal for the alcohols against 7.05 for diesel; boiling temperatures are 172.0°F for ethanol, 149.0°F for methanol, and a 376 to 627°F range for diesel; heats of vaporization are 396 and 502 Btu/lbm against about 110 for diesel. Because of those latent heats, vaporizing a stoichiometric amount of ethanol or methanol in air lowers the adiabatic mixture temperature by about 200°F and 356°F respectively, compared with roughly a 30°F drop for cetane — charge cooling that lengthens ignition delay in diesel combustion. The emissions instrumentation is documented in detail: a Beckman Model 955 heated chemiluminescent analyzer for oxides of nitrogen on a 550 ppm NO span gas, Beckman Models 864 and IR-15A infrared analyzers for CO and CO2, a Beckman Model 741 amperometric oxygen analyzer, and a Beckman Model 109 flame ionization detector for unburned hydrocarbons read as methane.

## 6. Two-dimensional analysis of two-phase reacting flow in a firing direct-injection diesel engine

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19900004076>

This paper extends spray and combustion modeling from the motored case to a firing engine. The two-dimensional two-phase formulation tracks the gas phase together with droplet parcels through injection, evaporation, ignition, and burn, so that the interaction between swirl, spray penetration, and heat release can be examined crank angle by crank angle. The report belongs to the same modeling family as the motored spray study in section 2, and the two are best read together: the motored work isolates the fluid mechanics of the spray, while this analysis adds chemistry and pressure rise. For engineers sizing a chamber or an injection schedule, the practical content is the demonstration of how ignition timing and flame structure respond to the swirl and spray parameters varied in the calculations.

## 7. Investigation of liquid fuel injection processes in a rotating detonation combustor using megahertz planar laser-induced fluorescence

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20210026164>

This recent NASA investigation applies megahertz-rate planar laser-induced fluorescence to a rotating detonation combustor, resolving liquid fuel injection and mixing at time scales short enough to follow the detonation wave. The diagnostic value of the work is the direct imaging of where liquid fuel actually resides between wave passages — the fraction that atomizes and mixes in time to burn in the wave, and the fraction that does not. As rotating detonation concepts move toward engine-relevant hardware, injection processes measured at this temporal resolution are the input that combustor design and model validation both require.

## 8. Investigation of spray dispersion and particulate formation in diesel fuel flames

Primary source: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19890010844>

This report studies how electrostatic charge modifies a fuel spray and the soot it forms. Charge is injected into the fuel from a cathode; the charged fuel forms a layer near the electrode, some charge is collected at the orifice electrode and some carried downstream. Breakdown occurs at higher voltages as chamber pressure rises — for example at about −8.5 kV at atmospheric back pressure, near −10 kV at 300 psig, and near −11 kV at 600 psig for the same flow rate — and after breakdown the emitted charge increases five to ten times. Charge density falls with increasing flow rate at fixed voltage, dropping from roughly 1 C/m3 at 0.51 m/s toward 0.3 C/m3 at 2.5 m/s at atmospheric pressure. The maximum charge on a drop is bounded by the Rayleigh limit, and measured drop size distributions sit close to that limit: at an injection velocity of 40 m/s the report gives d10 and d32 values of 234 and 241 micrometers against 367 and 384 micrometers for the comparison case. Increasing the field toward −12 kV opens the initial spray angle toward 180° and disperses the dense core; since soot forms primarily in fuel-rich regions such as the dense core, electrostatic dispersion of this kind should reduce soot formation. An appendix by K. Harstad and J. Bellan of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory treats electrostatic dispersion of drops in clusters, including characteristic evaporation time and volume expansion ratio against initial air/fuel mass ratio.

## 9. EPA diesel fuel standards

Primary source: <https://www.epa.gov/diesel-fuel-standards>

EPA's diesel fuel standards program page collects the regulations that reduced the sulfur content of diesel fuel used in transportation — the decades-long effort that enabled modern aftertreatment by removing the sulfur that poisons catalysts and particulate filters. The page is the entry point for the diesel fuel program rules, guidance, and related fuel quality requirements, and it links onward to the regulatory text. For compliance work it should be read together with the eCFR part 1036 text in section 3: the fuel standard and the engine standard are designed as a pair, and the fuel property tables used in emission correction assume fuel meeting the EPA diesel specifications.
